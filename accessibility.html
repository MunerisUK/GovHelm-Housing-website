Accessibility

The people who most need housing services are the most likely to be excluded by a badly built one. Accessibility gates a release here, rather than following one.

  • WCAG 2.2 level AA target
  • Tested with assistive technology
  • Public sector duty supported

Our commitment

IHAP aims to conform to WCAG 2.2 level AA across both the citizen portal and the staff workspace. Staff interfaces are included deliberately: disabled people work in housing teams, and an inaccessible back office quietly excludes them from the job.

Conformance is checked in three ways. Automated checks run on every change and block the build on failure. Manual keyboard and screen reader testing runs against each release candidate. And periodic testing with disabled users is commissioned for significant new journeys.

What this means in practice

Pages are server-rendered HTML that works before JavaScript arrives and continues to work if it never does. Forms use native controls with visible labels and error summaries that move focus. Nothing depends on colour alone, hover, or a pointing device. Content is written to be read, with a plain English review on every resident-facing string.

Long forms save as you go, because a session timeout that loses forty minutes of work is an accessibility failure whatever the audit tool says.

Testing

Each release candidate is tested against the combinations below. Results, including known issues, are published with the release.

Standards we work to

  • WCAG 2.2 level AA
  • Public Sector Bodies (Websites and Mobile Applications) Accessibility Regulations 2018
  • Equality Act 2010 reasonable adjustments
  • EN 301 549 for procurement
  • GOV.UK Service Manual patterns where applicable

Model accessibility statement

Publishing an accessibility statement is a legal requirement for public sector bodies. A model statement covering the platform's known position is supplied with the licence, for you to adapt to your own deployment and testing.

Automated testing is a floor, not a ceiling

Automated tooling catches perhaps a third of real accessibility problems. It runs on every change because it is cheap, not because it is sufficient. The manual and user testing above is what actually holds the standard.

Inclusive design beyond WCAG

Things the standard does not require but people need

Assisted digital

Every online journey has an officer-keyed equivalent, so a resident who cannot use the portal is not pushed to the back of the queue.

Plain English

Resident-facing content is written at a reading age of nine to eleven and reviewed by the service, not only by developers.

Translation

Content is externalised for translation with a workflow for community languages, and the interface supports right-to-left scripts.

Low bandwidth

Pages are small and work on old devices and poor connections, which describes a great many applicants.

Trauma-informed journeys

Sensitive questions are asked once, explained, and never repeated unnecessarily across services.

Session resilience

Work is saved continuously. A timeout, a dropped connection or a closed tab does not cost the applicant their progress.

Found a barrier?

Tell us, and it will be treated as a defect

Accessibility issues are logged as defects with the same priority as anything else that stops someone completing a task, and fixing one is never a chargeable change request. If you hit a barrier in the portal or the staff workspace, tell us. Please say what you were trying to do, what happened, and what assistive technology you were using.

If you are a resident, report the problem to your housing provider as well: they have a duty to make reasonable adjustments while the underlying defect is fixed.
