Deployment scenarios

Four worked scenarios showing how IHAP fits different kinds of housing provider, what the implementation involves and where the difficulty usually sits.

These are illustrative scenarios, not customer references

IHAP is pre-release, and the scenarios below are composites written to show how the platform is intended to be adopted. They are not accounts of named organisations, and the figures are planning assumptions rather than measured results.

As providers go live and are willing to be named, this page will be replaced with real case studies. We would rather show you a labelled illustration than an unlabelled invention. If you are running IHAP and would like to share what happened, please get in touch.

Scenario one

A metropolitan borough replacing a housing register

Around 9,000 households on the register, 14,000 council-owned units, a homelessness service under sustained pressure and an incumbent system at the end of its contract.

The problem

The register, the homelessness case system and the tenancy system were three separate products from two suppliers. Applicants were routinely duplicated across them. The H-CLIC return took a fortnight of manual reconciliation each quarter, and the allocations scheme had been amended four times without the system being updated to match.

The approach

Register first. Applications, assessment and allocations went live together after a six-month implementation, with the tenancy system left in place and fed through the integrations hub. Homelessness casework followed five months later, once officers trusted the data.

The difficult part

Not the software. Agreeing the current allocation scheme in writing, precisely enough to encode as rules, took eleven weeks and surfaced three points where practice had quietly diverged from published policy.

What changed

One applicant record across services. Banding decisions carrying a written reason and a scheme version. The quarterly return assembled from live case data and validated before submission rather than after.

Shape of the deployment

  • 9,000 households on register
  • 6 months to first go-live
  • 3 systems consolidated to 1
  • Tenancy system retained

Scenario two

A housing association standardising lettings across regions

A registered provider with 22,000 homes across three regions, each with its own lettings practice inherited from historic mergers, and nominations arriving from eleven local authorities.

The problem

Three regions ran three versions of the same process. Nominations arrived by spreadsheet, email and one partner's portal. Void turnaround varied by a fortnight between regions and nobody could explain why with data.

The approach

Allocations, lettings and tenancy went live in one region as a pilot, then rolled to the others at quarterly intervals. The integrations hub took nominations from partner authorities over the API where they could support it and by scheduled file transfer where they could not.

The difficult part

Partner readiness. Standardising internally was straightforward; agreeing a nomination format with eleven authorities on different systems was the long pole, and the file-transfer fallback earned its place.

What changed

One lettings process with documented regional variations as configuration rather than as local custom. Void turnaround comparable between regions for the first time. Nominations tracked end to end with an auditable outcome for each.

Shape of the deployment

  • 22,000 homes
  • 3 regions on one process
  • 11 nominating authorities
  • Quarterly phased rollout

Scenario three

A district council under homelessness pressure

A smaller district with no housing stock of its own, a statutory homelessness duty, temporary accommodation costs rising faster than the budget, and a team of nine.

The problem

Casework ran on a general-purpose case management product that knew nothing about the Homelessness Reduction Act. Duty deadlines were tracked in a shared spreadsheet. Temporary accommodation placements and charges were reconciled monthly, by hand, against invoices.

The approach

Homelessness casework first, on the managed services topology, with the register following six months later. No stock meant no tenancy or income modules, which kept the implementation small.

The difficult part

Historic case data. Five years of casework in free-text notes had to be loaded with enough structure to answer a review or an ombudsman enquiry, without pretending to a precision the source did not have.

What changed

Duty clocks tracked by the platform with alerts before expiry. Personalised Housing Plans produced from case data rather than retyped. Temporary accommodation placements and charges reconciled continuously instead of monthly.

Shape of the deployment

  • No stock, statutory duty only
  • 9-person team
  • 4-month implementation
  • 2 modules deployed

Scenario four

A sub-regional choice-based lettings partnership

Six authorities and four housing associations presenting a single choice-based lettings scheme to residents across a travel-to-work area.

The problem

One public-facing scheme, ten back-office systems. Residents saw a shared advert site but had to deal with each landlord separately after bidding. Cross-boundary reporting was assembled by hand from ten exports that never quite agreed.

The approach

A shared IHAP instance for the lettings and advert modules, with each member retaining its own tenancy and income systems and connecting through the integrations hub. Partner-scoped access means each member sees its own stock and the shared applicant pool.

The difficult part

Governance, not technology. Agreeing one scheme, one set of quotas and one definition of local connection across ten organisations took longer than the build, and the partnership agreement had to be rewritten to say who could change what.

What changed

One scheme, genuinely shared. Residents bid once and see one position. Cross-boundary reporting produced from one dataset, and the cost of running the scheme shared rather than duplicated ten times.

Shape of the deployment

  • 10 partner organisations
  • 1 shared scheme
  • Partner-scoped access
  • Members keep their own systems

What these have in common

Four patterns worth noticing

The software is rarely the hard part

In every scenario the long pole is agreement: the scheme, the partnership, the data mapping. Budget for that, not for the install.

Phasing beats a big bang

Each scenario goes live with the modules that hurt most and extends outwards once officers trust the data.

Existing systems stay

Nobody replaced their finance or asset system. The integrations hub exists precisely so that they do not have to, and it is part of the licence rather than a chargeable connector per system.

Data quality surfaces early

Profiling the incumbent data before agreeing a plan is what separates a six-month implementation from an eighteen-month one.

Thinking about a deployment?

We would rather talk about your data quality and your allocation scheme than give you a slide deck. Those are the things that decide how this goes.
